# The Missing Greek: what settlement risk actually costs on prediction markets

*Marco Amendola, July 2026. On-chain numbers reconstructed from public Polygon RPC; method, coverage audit and artifacts: github.com/x-pertknight/traptrade. A note on the title: strictly speaking what this piece computes is a valuation adjustment, a settlement CVA, not a sensitivity. "Greek" is shorthand for a risk axis that belongs in the quote and today is not there.*

Binary options have delta, theta and vega. On-chain binary options carry a fourth risk that nobody quotes: the chance that the settlement mechanism itself reprices your position after the event is over. This piece measures it, and the measurement kills the number everyone quotes.

## The setup

Prediction markets are doing somewhere between $20B and $30B a month across venues, depending on whose estimate and whose perimeter you take, and the market-structure side is well mapped. What stays unquantified is oracle risk: every treatment I have found handles resolution qualitatively. This is the quantification.

I reconstructed Polymarket resolutions and UMA disputes from public Polygon RPC (event logs and DVM vote receipts, joined wallet by wallet; no API keys, no indexer trust). Resolution events cover January 2025 through July 2026; the dispute dataset with full vote reconstruction covers April 2025 onward, so all rates below are computed on the April 2025 to June 2026 window. One thing that window choice must not hide: March 2025 contains the best-known UMA failure on Polymarket, the "Ukraine mineral deal" market, where large stakers voted through a contested outcome. It predates the reconstructed-dispute window, it is not in these base rates, and it matters for the tail discussion below.

## First, the number this piece retracts from its own draft

An earlier version of this analysis led with a stat that survives every arithmetic check and fails the economic one: when a Polymarket dispute reaches UMA's DVM, the disputer wins 74.7% of the time (727 of 973 disputes reconstructed on the classic adapters). That number is real. It is also, for a position holder, wrong by roughly 5x, and anyone quoting it as loss frequency is mispricing the risk.

Decompose the 727 "overturns" by what the DVM actually said. 656 of them, 90%, settled at UMA's magic "too early" price: the vote did not rule the proposal wrong, it ruled it premature. The question resets and gets re-proposed. Only 67 were direct flips to the opposite outcome, and 4 settled at 0.5.

A too-early is not a loss. It is a delay with a re-roll. So I joined all 656 too-early disputes to the final on-chain resolution of their question (by questionID; 648 of 656 had resolved by the scan cutoff). The result: 577 resolved to exactly the outcome originally proposed, 69 flipped, 2 had 0.5 proposals themselves (one finalized at 0.5, one at 0), and 8 are still pending. The median too-early question reached its final outcome about 6 hours after the dispute; 81% within a week. The tail is real though: when the outcome did flip, the median wait was 4.8 days, the p95 about 80 days, and the worst case took 226 days.

Put the direct flips and the post-reset flips together and the number that matters comes out: **conditional on a dispute, the final outcome differs from the original proposal 14.0% of the time** (136 of 973; one ambiguous case with a 0.5 proposal excluded, counting it makes it 137). For a holder of the proposed side at 98 cents, expected terminal loss conditional on dispute is about 14 cents, not the 73 cents the raw overturn rate implies. The most-quoted risk number in this corner of the market overstates the economic risk by about 5x. That is the headline finding, and it cuts in Polymarket's favor.

## The base rates

912,987 questions resolved across the UMA adapter generations in the window. Two structural facts before any rate gets quoted.

First, this is not the whole venue anymore. Since September 2025 Polymarket also settles markets outside UMA entirely: in a random-sample audit of June and July 2026 resolution events on the conditional tokens contract (135,126 events), UMA-book adapters accounted for 70.2% (58.2% the newer managed adapter, 12.0% via the negRisk adapter), a Chainlink-based resolver for 21.0% (the automated crypto-price markets, live since the September 2025 Chainlink Data Streams integration), and, worth pausing on, a plain externally-owned account, a signer key with no contract code, directly resolved 6.8%. One subtlety on the negRisk slice, found in this piece's own audit: the UMA dispute right there lives at the question level, and one resolved question fans out into many sibling condition resolutions, settled in bulk transactions with no per-condition UMA event (sampled bulk txs resolve up to 15 conditions each). Counting conditions, negRisk is 12% of resolutions; counting UMA questions it is far smaller, and the condition-to-question ratio is tracked in the repo audit. Every rate in this piece is a rate on the UMA books. The Chainlink book has a different risk model (deterministic feeds, no dispute path), and the EOA book is pure centralized settlement: no oracle, no dispute right, one key. Nobody should quote a single "venue" dispute rate, including me.

Second, the UMA side is two different books wearing one brand:

- **The classic book** (long-dated, human-judged questions): the dispute rate depends on the regime you measure. In the clean pre-migration window (April to August 2025): 406 DVM disputes on 27,375 resolutions, **1.48%**. Post-migration (September 2025 to June 2026): 553 on 90,566, **0.61%**. Full window: 0.81%. The decline is a mechanism change, not noise: in August 2025 UMA governance moved Polymarket proposals to a managed oracle with whitelisted proposers (UMIP-189, 37 addresses; the right to dispute stayed open to anyone). Whitelisted proposers make fewer bad proposals. The honest quote is the range, 0.6% to 1.5%, with the regime attached.
- **The managed book** (the newer adapter, live since September 2025, now 130-280k resolutions a month): first-level dispute rate 0.06-0.31% monthly. And here is the correction this piece almost needed a fourth time. This book is not just automated price questions anymore: new human-judged markets launch on it too, and its dispute path runs through a managed optimistic oracle rather than the open one. That oracle shows 1,889 disputes since launch, 1,517 from the main adapter, roughly 0.16% of its resolutions. The two loudest Polymarket fights of 2026, the $16M Clavicular market (two dispute rounds) and the $200M+ US-Iran ceasefire-extension market (three rounds), both lived on this path, and both were ultimately decided by UMA token voters. My round-by-round vote reconstruction covers the classic adapters only, so I quote no overturn or flip rate for this book: that reconstruction is the open frontier in the repo audit. Anyone applying my 14% to the ceasefire market is making the same category of mistake this piece opens by retracting.

When a DVM dispute lands on the classic book, the clock: 3.25 days median from dispute to DVM settlement (p95 4.2, max 16.4, pooled across all 973). But as the decomposition shows, for 90% of disputes the market's own question typically re-resolves within hours, before the DVM vote even lands. Settlement is delayed, not capital frozen: the tokens keep trading throughout.

And the flip test, unchanged from the earlier draft because it survived every audit: **zero of 939 historical outcomes** would have flipped even reassigning every measured conflicted voter's stake to their own book (max conflicted stake ever observed: 1.30% of the final round, against the ~50% a flip needs). Read it precisely: no historical outcome was moved through the measured wallet-level conflict channel. It is not a security proof. The prospective attack, a whale acquiring stake and position together, is exactly the March 2025 pattern, and the flip test does not bound it. Full counterfactual in the flip-test writeup.

Reconciliation, since rates live and die on denominators: 973 disputes reconstructed (through July 2026), 959 inside the windowed rates (through June 2026), 939 joined to complete DVM vote data for the flip test, 406 inside the era-clean classic window, 656 too-early disputes of which 648 joined to a final resolution.

## The decomposition

Call S the settlement adjustment: the discount a rational holder applies to a $1 claim for settlement risk alone. Computed on the classic book, for the exposed pattern: buying the proposed-winning side near $1 after a proposal.

**Delay component.** Frequency times funding cost of delayed settlement. Even priced on the DVM path's 3.25 days at r = 5%, this is about 0.07 basis points. On the 6-hour median of the too-early path it vanishes entirely. Anyone modeling settlement risk as a delay cost has measured the wrong thing.

**Repricing component.** Conditional on dispute, the final outcome differs 14.0% of the time, so a holder at 98 cents expects to lose about 14 cents conditional on dispute. Unconditionally: dispute rate x 0.139. On the current regime (0.61%): **about 8.5 bps of notional**. On the clean pre-migration regime (1.48%): about 21 bps. Call it 8 to 20 bps depending on which era you are pricing, concentrated in an event that changes your outcome roughly once per 500 to 1,200 markets held.

For the record: the earlier draft of this piece put this number at 105-110 bps, by treating every DVM overturn as a terminal loss. The preregistered prediction, logged before any data was pulled, was 1-5 bps. The corrected number lands between the two, closer to the prereg than to the draft. The prereg file, with the wrong guesses left in, is in the repo.

Three caveats that survive the correction. First, these are cross-market base rates, not bounds, and they are biased upward for exactly the markets that trade at 98 cents: disputes select ambiguous proposals, and ambiguous markets do not trade at 98. Condition on the market's own price and S shrinks for clean markets, grows for rules-lawyer markets. Second, the dispute rate is a floor on proposal error: a wrong proposal on a market too small to be worth the dispute bond never enters these numbers, and the holder eats it anyway. S prices disputed misresolution; undisputed misresolution is unmeasured and additive. Third, a unit warning: these are per-question rates, and negRisk markets split one market into several questions; the notional-weighted rate a market maker actually wants would weight by open interest, which this piece does not have.

## The benchmark nobody can compute

The natural comparison is Kalshi, the CFTC-regulated venue. Here the asymmetry is the finding.

In the normal case both venues settle in hours. The difference is the exception path. UMA's is slow when it escalates, adversarial, economically incentivized, and fully observable: every number above replays from the repo. Kalshi's is fast on paper (Rule 7.1(b) of the CFTC-filed rulebook gives the Outcome Review Committee 24 hours to determine a final outcome) but initiated at Kalshi's sole discretion, decided by an internal committee, and final by Rule 7.1(c). There is no rule-defined adversarial dispute right, and no dispute-rate statistics are published.

The 2026 record, offered as existence proofs and not as rates, shows what discretionary-and-final looks like under stress. The Cardi B halftime question: the specific "will she perform" contract carried about $4.4M, inside a halftime-performer complex that did over $47M, and Kalshi settled at the last traded price by invoking Rule 6.3(c), with a CFTC complaint to show for it. The $54M Khamenei market settled at pre-strike prices and produced a class action and fee reimbursements; Kalshi maintains the death carveout was already in its CFTC product filing, and it was, but the practice was codified into the public rulebook only afterwards, in March 2026, and the litigation contests the framing. An NFL win-total mis-grade was corrected only after a viral post. Correction by public pressure and litigation is a dispute mechanism too. It has real teeth. What it does not have is a base rate you can put in a model.

So the trade-off is not "risky oracle vs safe regulator". It is a measurable exception path vs an unmeasurable one. For a market maker, measurable risk is pricable risk. That is the missing Greek: on one venue you can compute it, on the other you can only hope it stays small.

## Three regimes, two oracles and a signer key

The adapter migration is not a footnote, it is the direction of travel. Polymarket in mid-2026 is at least three settlement regimes: a classic UMA book, fully reconstructable, where disputes run 0.6-1.5% and this piece's 14% and 8-20 bps live; a managed book (UMA-managed plus Chainlink-automated) where first-level disputes run an order of magnitude rarer per question, but where the biggest fights of 2026 actually landed, behind an oracle whose round-by-round record I have not yet reconstructed; and a directly-resolved slice settled by a key, with no oracle at all. The direction of travel is worth saying out loud: Polymarket's growth is moving settlement from the most measurable of its three regimes toward the two less measurable ones. Pricing settlement risk starts with knowing which book you are in, and the honest version of this analysis can only fully price the first.

## Limitations, stated plainly

The classic-book regime split is confirmed at the questionID level for disputes (every reconstructed dispute traces to the classic adapters), but the 1.48% extrapolates a 5-month clean window. The too-early join covers 648 of 656 disputes; the 8 pending could nudge the 14.0% by a few tenths. The loss-given-flip assumes terminal value zero, an upper bound. The wallet-level conflict measurement misses proxy and off-chain exposure, and says nothing about prospective stake acquisition. The oracle-coverage audit samples June-July 2026; the non-UMA share was smaller in earlier months, and the split over time is in the repo. The managed book's 1,889 disputes are counted, not reconstructed: no overturn, flip, or latency figure in this piece applies to that book. Kalshi's side of the comparison is qualitative by necessity, which is itself the point. And S is a valuation adjustment with stated assumptions, not a trading signal.
